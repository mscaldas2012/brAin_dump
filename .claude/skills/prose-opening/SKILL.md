# Prose Opening

Use this skill when reviewing the first paragraph or paragraphs of any prose piece — essay, article, chapter, blog post, nonfiction book section. The opening's sole obligation is to begin: to establish the subject, the voice, and the reason to continue reading, without first warming up.

**Minimum text unit:** First 5 paragraphs (or up to 500 words, whichever is shorter). For long texts (20,000+ words), this skill always reads the actual opening of the full text, not the skeleton — opening faults cannot be diagnosed by proxy.

Source concept: "The most essential function and special excellence of the proem is to make clear what is the end or purpose" of the piece. (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Book III Ch. 14.) The corollary from Quiller-Couch: "The first rule of style is to have something to say; and the second rule is to say it." The opening that announces what it will say before it says it has spent its only first impression on a rehearsal. (Quiller-Couch, *On the Art of Writing*, Lecture XII, 1916.) Fowler's diagnostic: every word in the opening must be "direct, simple, brief, vigorous" — words that stall the arrival of the subject fail all four. (Fowler & Fowler, *The King's English*, 1908.)

## Fault Taxonomy

Six named faults, checked in order.

### Fault 1 — Throat-Clearing
Warm-up sentences that state context, acknowledge the topic's importance, or gesture toward the subject before the subject itself arrives. The subject of the first sentence is not the subject of the piece.

*Signs:* "For many years…", "In today's world…", "It has long been recognized…", "Scholars have debated…", "This is a question that…"

*Test:* Delete the first sentence. If the piece loses no claim, image, or information — only atmosphere — the first sentence is throat-clearing.

### Fault 2 — Delayed Arrival
The subject does not appear until the second or third paragraph. The first paragraph (or first two) is scene-setting, context, or framing that has not earned its position at the front.

*Test:* Cross out the first paragraph. If the piece makes full sense, the first paragraph was approach, not beginning.

### Fault 3 — Summary Opener
The opening announces what the piece will argue or cover rather than beginning to argue or cover it. The reader is told what is coming rather than shown it arriving.

*Signs:* "In this essay, I will argue…", "This piece examines three factors…", "What follows is an analysis of…", "I want to make three points about…"

### Fault 4 — Rhetorical Question Opener
The opening poses a question the writer intends to answer — using the question as a proxy for making a claim. This avoids the commitment of beginning with the thing.

*Signs:* "Have you ever wondered why…?", "What makes X so powerful?", "Why does Y matter?"

*Exception:* A question is acceptable when the piece is genuinely organized around a question it does not resolve, and the opening makes this structure clear — not as a hook, but as an honest thesis.

### Fault 5 — Definition Opener
The opening begins with a dictionary or encyclopedic definition of the central subject. Throat-clearing in a specific costume.

*Signs:* "According to the Oxford English Dictionary…", "The word 'X' derives from the Latin…", "By 'democracy,' I mean…" (where the definition does no essential work).

*Exception:* A definition earns its position when the piece's central argument hinges on a distinction in meaning that must be precisely established before the argument can proceed — and when the definition is the argument's first move, not its preamble.

### Fault 6 — Unearned Anecdote
The opening uses a personal or third-party anecdote that does not connect to or earn the central subject — an attempt to create warmth or engagement before the piece has established what the reader is being engaged in.

*Signs:* An anecdote that ends with a pivot ("This made me think about X…") that reveals it was a vehicle, not a beginning. An anecdote that could be removed without affecting the first actual claim.

*Exception:* An anecdote is earned when the anecdote is the subject — when the piece is about the event or the person described, not about an abstraction the anecdote is meant to introduce.

## Objective Rubric

- The first sentence contains or immediately implies the central subject of the piece.
- No sentence in the first paragraph could be removed without losing a claim, image, or essential piece of information.
- The opening contains no announcement of what the piece will argue — it begins to argue.
- No rhetorical question serves as a substitute for a claim.
- Any anecdote in the opening is about the subject, not an approach to the subject.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
