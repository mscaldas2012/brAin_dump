# English Economy of Figures

Use this skill when figurative language is less efficient than it could be — similes spelled out where metaphors would do, extended analogies that explain themselves, or figures that take more words than the literal statement they replace.

Source concept: Figures of speech derive their value from economy — they convey a complex relationship in fewer words or less cognitive effort than a literal paraphrase would require. A simile makes the comparison explicit; a metaphor fuses it; an implied or embedded metaphor works without naming the figure at all. Use the most compressed form the reader can follow without effort. Where no figure is more economical than the literal, use the literal. (Spencer, *The Philosophy of Style*, 1852)

## Rules

- **The compression ladder** (most to least economical): **implied/embedded metaphor** (*"the root of the argument"* — the figure is built into the noun) → **stated metaphor** (*"the argument is a tree"*) → **simile** (*"the argument is like a tree"*) → **extended analogy** (*"just as a tree grows from a single seed and ramifies into branches, so this argument..."*). Use the highest rung the reader can follow without stumbling.
- **Over-explained simile**: a simile that states the comparison and then explains it is an extended analogy wearing a simile's clothes. *"His anger swept through the room like a storm — just as a storm darkens the sky and batters everything in its path, so his anger clouded every conversation and left everyone shaken."* Compress: *"His anger swept through the room"* (implied metaphor) or, if the image still needs establishing, *"His anger was a storm."* (stated metaphor). Cut the explanatory tail.
- **The redundant-gloss test**: if the sentence immediately following a figure restates what the figure means, the figure is over-explained. Cut the gloss and let the figure work, or cut the figure and keep the gloss as plain statement. Never keep both.
- **Plain statement beats a costly figure**: if establishing a figure takes more words than a direct literal description and adds nothing beyond the slight vividness of the comparison, use plain statement. A simile that requires a full sentence to set up and a second to cash out costs more than it saves; *"she was very angry"* is more economical than the failed analogy.
- Distinguish from `english-dying-metaphors`: that skill asks whether a figure retains visual power. This skill asks whether the writer has chosen the most economical level of compression. A fresh, vivid simile may still be less economical than the equivalent metaphor; the question here is not freshness but compression.

## Objective Rubric

- No simile in the revision could be replaced by a stated metaphor without loss of clarity to this reader for this comparison.
- No stated metaphor could be replaced by an implied/embedded form without loss of clarity.
- No figure is followed by an immediate restatement of its meaning (no redundant gloss).
- No figure takes more words than a plain literal statement of the same content unless the figurative form provides genuine vividness or economy the plain form cannot match.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.

## Source Boundary

Spencer's *The Philosophy of Style* (1852) is in the public domain. Examples should be drawn from public-domain English prose (pre-1928) or invented passages clearly labeled as such. Do not invent quotations attributed to named authors. Note: for figures that have lost visual power, see `english-dying-metaphors`; for figures that are visually incompatible with each other, see `english-mixed-metaphors`.
