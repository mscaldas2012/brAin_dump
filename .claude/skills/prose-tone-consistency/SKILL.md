# Prose Tone Consistency

Use this skill when a piece feels uneasy without being obviously broken — when the writing seems to belong to several different pieces, or when a section jars against its neighbors not for what it argues but for how it sounds. The fault is not in any single section taken alone but in the register relationship between sections.

**Minimum text unit:** One sample of 150–200 words from each major section. For Tier 1–2 (under 20,000 words): sample from the opening of the piece, the midpoint of each major section, and the close. For Tier 3 (over 20,000 words): one sample per chapter, drawn from that chapter's midpoint. This skill operates entirely on samples; the full body of each section does not need to be read.

Source concept: "The style to be good must be, first of all, clear; it must also be appropriate… it is important that the language should not be out of tune with the subject." (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Book III Ch. 7.) Aristotle's term is *to prepon* — propriety of style — the requirement that the manner of writing fit the subject, the audience, and the moment. A piece that maintains propriety throughout feels coherent; one that violates it feels scattered even when the individual sections are well-written. Fowler grounds the principle in the writer's five vocabulary rules: the appropriate word is familiar, concrete, brief, and direct. (Fowler & Fowler, *The King's English*, Chapter I, 1908.) Systematic deviation from this baseline in isolated sections — bursts of latinate formality in an otherwise plain piece, or casual informality in an otherwise elevated one — marks a register fault. Quiller-Couch establishes appropriateness as the first cardinal virtue of prose: "the root of the matter" from which all other virtues follow. (*On the Art of Writing*, Lecture II, 1916.)

## Register Profile

Before comparing samples, characterize each along four dimensions:

**Formality** — how formal is the diction and sentence structure?
- *Casual*: contractions, short sentences, direct address, colloquial phrasing
- *Conversational*: clear and direct, but without contractions or slang; speaks to the reader as a person
- *Formal*: structured, complete sentences; more distanced; avoids colloquialism
- *Academic*: specialised vocabulary, complex syntax, hedging apparatus, impersonal constructions

**Intimacy** — what is the distance between writer and reader?
- *Distanced*: the writer is not visible; the prose is impersonal
- *Neutral*: the writer is present but not foregrounded
- *Personal*: first person is active; the writer's experience and perspective are part of the argument
- *Confessional*: the writer is emotionally exposed; the reader is invited into private territory

**Authority** — how confident is the writer's stance?
- *Tentative*: many qualifications, explicit uncertainty, deferral to other sources
- *Hedged*: qualified but directional; the writer has views but holds them lightly
- *Balanced*: asserts with some qualification; acknowledges complexity without retreating from claims
- *Assertive*: makes claims directly and stands behind them; qualifications appear only where genuinely warranted

**Vocabulary level** — what is the dominant level of the diction?
- *Everyday*: plain, common, Anglo-Saxon words predominate
- *Mixed*: everyday and elevated words in proportion
- *Elevated*: latinate, polysyllabic, or technical vocabulary predominates
- *Specialised*: domain-specific terminology; assumes a specific reader

Record the register profile of each sample as four comma-separated terms, e.g.:
`conversational · personal · assertive · mixed`

## Baseline and Shift Detection

Once all samples are profiled, identify the **register baseline** — the profile established in the opening sample. This is the register the reader assumes the piece will maintain.

A shift is worth flagging when:
- Any dimension changes by more than one position (e.g., *conversational* → *academic*; *personal* → *distanced*)
- The shift persists for a full section without returning
- The shift is not explained by the content of that section (a section discussing the author's childhood warrants more *personal* intimacy; a section presenting statistical evidence warrants more *hedged* authority — these are not faults)

A shift is **earned** when: the content of the section genuinely calls for a different register, and the shift is signaled (by a transition, a change in addressee, a structural break, or a clear change of subject).

A shift is **unearned** when: the register changes without a structural reason and without a signal to the reader.

## Fault Taxonomy

Five named faults.

### Fault 1 — Register Break
A sudden, localized shift in register between adjacent sections that is not earned by the content and not signaled to the reader. The register returns after the section ends — the shift is not the start of a sustained change. Identifiable by: one sample that differs markedly from its neighbors on both sides.

*Test:* If the anomalous section were removed, would the surrounding sections' registers connect smoothly? If yes, the section is a register break — an island of a different voice in an otherwise consistent piece.

*Distinction:* A break is localized; it is not a decision to shift register for the rest of the piece, only a section that slipped out of the established register and back.

### Fault 2 — Register Drift
Gradual, unacknowledged migration of register across the whole piece. No single section breaks sharply; instead, each section is slightly different from the last, and the accumulated shift from opening to close is substantial. The ending is in a materially different register from the opening, but no deliberate shift was made.

*Test:* Compare the opening sample's profile to the closing sample's profile. If they differ on two or more dimensions, trace when each dimension shifted. If no individual transition can be identified as a deliberate register shift, the piece has drifted.

*Distinction:* Drift is directional and cumulative; a break is localized and returns. Both are faults, but drift is harder to spot and harder to repair — it requires identifying where the migration began and pulling the later sections back to the baseline.

### Fault 3 — Unearned Intimacy
A sudden shift to a more intimate or confessional register — *personal* or *confessional* on the intimacy dimension — in a piece otherwise operating at *neutral* or *distanced*. The reader is not prepared for the closeness. The writer moves to emotional exposure without structural cause.

*Test:* Does the content of the section warrant the intimacy? A personal narrative, a confession of uncertainty, an account of experience — these can earn intimacy. A sudden intimate aside in a technical argument does not. Ask: if a reader unfamiliar with the writer encountered this section first, would they expect the surrounding piece to be in the same register? If not, the intimacy is unearned.

*Distinction:* A piece that establishes a *personal* or *confessional* baseline in the opening and maintains it is not committing this fault. The fault is the sudden shift from a distanced or neutral register to an intimate one, without preparation.

### Fault 4 — Vocabulary Inconsistency
The vocabulary level shifts markedly between sections in ways the register does not otherwise require. One section uses *utilise*, *aforementioned*, *subsequent to*, *pertaining to*; an adjacent section uses *messy*, *flat-out wrong*, *dead wrong*, *get at*. The inconsistency is not a tonal decision — it is not serving formality, intimacy, or authority — it is simply uncontrolled variation.

*Test:* List the five most distinctive vocabulary choices in each sample. Compare lists across adjacent samples. If adjacent samples' word lists come from different registers without any register-level explanation for the difference, the vocabulary is inconsistent.

*Distinction:* Deliberate register code-switching — using plain language to punch through after elevated argument — can be a valid rhetorical move. The fault is inconsistency that tracks nothing: the vocabulary level varies without serving any purpose the surrounding register supports.

### Fault 5 — Authority Slip
The piece's implied level of confidence shifts between sections without tracking the actual certainty of the content. A section making a confident claim (*assertive* authority) is followed by a section making the same strength of claim with heavy qualification (*tentative* authority), not because the second claim is genuinely more uncertain but because the writer is less comfortable with the material.

*Test:* For the sections that differ in authority level: is the more hedged section making a genuinely more uncertain claim? If yes, the hedging is earned. If both sections are making claims of comparable certainty, and one is hedged while the other is assertive, the authority is slipping — the writer's confidence, not the argument's uncertainty, is driving the qualification.

*Distinction:* Appropriate epistemic humility — acknowledging genuine uncertainty, noting where evidence is thin — is not an authority slip. The fault is asymmetric hedging: hedging some claims heavily while asserting equally uncertain claims confidently, without a principled reason for the asymmetry.

## Objective Rubric

- Every major section has a sample and a register profile — no section omitted.
- The register baseline is stated from the opening sample before comparison begins.
- Each fault names the specific dimension that shifted, the location, and the test result.
- The consistency assessment distinguishes earned shifts (content-driven, signaled) from unearned ones.
- The priority repair either returns the anomalous section to the baseline register or explicitly earns the shift through signal and structural cause.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
