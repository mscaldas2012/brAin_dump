What novelists can teach us about time

Fiction writers have always understood something that business writers tend to miss: time is not neutral on the page. A sentence can last a paragraph. A decade can vanish in a clause. The manipulation of pace — speeding through the forgettable, lingering on the charged — is one of the most powerful tools in a writer's kit.

Non-fiction writers often forget this. They write chronologically because events happened chronologically, which is a perfectly reasonable approach if your goal is to be accurate and completely unreadable. The reader doesn't experience time the way it happened. They experience the time of reading, which is different, and which the writer controls entirely.

It's worth noting that this is why memoir is so much harder than it looks. The memoirist must navigate two timelines simultaneously: the time of the events, and the time of the telling, with the narrator's present-day understanding casting its shadow backward. Novelists do this instinctively; essayists and journalists often don't.

Consider what happens when a writer lingers. Proust's madeleine sequence — essentially a page spent on a bite of cake — works because the reader is made to slow down alongside the narrator. The prose enacts what it describes. This is not merely a nuanced technique; it is fundamentally how the form communicates.

Moreover, the reverse is equally powerful. A chapter that covers fifteen years in three sentences isn't lazy — it's a statement. Those years didn't matter, or they mattered so much that the narrator can't yet touch them. The speed is the meaning.

The practical takeaway: when you're writing anything long, ask where you're going slow and where you're going fast, and ask whether those choices are deliberate. Most writers default to a kind of mid-tempo cruise that smooths over everything equally. The result is prose that is thorough, comprehensive, and utterly unmemorable.
