Notes on revision

The first draft is for finding out what you're writing about. This is Joan Didion's idea, or something like it, and it's the most useful thing I know about the work.

Most writers treat the first draft as a rough version of the final thing. They try to get it right — correct sentences, working arguments, the right word. This is almost always a mistake. It makes the writing slow and anxious, and worse, it closes off possibilities before you've had a chance to discover them. You can't revise your way out of the wrong idea. You have to write your way to the right one.

The second draft is where the real decisions get made. By then you know what survives. You know which sentences were there to get you to the next sentence, and which ones you'd actually fight for. You can delete the scaffolding.

Professional writers tend to revise more than beginners, not less. Beginners think revision is what you do when something went wrong. Professionals know revision is the work. The first draft just gives you something to revise.

One concrete thing: read your draft aloud before you touch it. The ear catches what the eye misses — the sentence that runs out of steam halfway through, the word that keeps appearing, the paragraph that's doing the same job as the paragraph before it. You don't need an editor for this. You need fifteen minutes and a quiet room.

The other concrete thing: cut the first paragraph. Not always, but often enough that it's worth trying. Writers warm up on the page the same way speakers clear their throats. The real opening is usually the second paragraph.
