# English Wardour Street

Use this skill when prose is sprinkled with archaic words or phrases not because the writing is committed to a historical register throughout, but because the writer has reached for them to seem literary, elevated, or atmospheric.

Source concept: Wardour-Street English is prose furnished with archaisms in the manner of a theatrical prop-house: the words are old-fashioned reproductions, not the genuine article, and their presence marks the writing as affected rather than authentic. Named after the London street known for its dealers in antique and reproduction furniture. (Fowler & Fowler, *The King's English*, 1908)

## Rules

- An archaism is a word or phrase no longer in ordinary use that the writer has introduced for stylistic effect. Common Wardour-Street words and forms: *forsooth, methinks, ere, prithee, perchance, mayhap, betimes, anon, certes, marry, wot, hight, yclept, natheless, eftsoons, whilom, withal, howbeit,* and contracted pseudo-antique forms: *'tis, 'twas, 'twere, 'twould.*
- **The register test**: is the writing working in a genuinely and consistently archaic register throughout — sustained historical fiction, deliberate pastiche, formal liturgical prose — such that the archaic word belongs to the whole? If yes, the archaism is legitimate. If the prose is otherwise modern and the archaic word appears as an isolated ornament, it is Wardour Street.
- **The substitution test**: replace the archaism with its modern equivalent. Is anything lost beyond the pseudo-antique flavour? If not, use the modern form or cut entirely. *"Ere long"* → *"soon"*; *"methinks"* → *"I think"* or cut; *"forsooth"* → *"indeed"* or cut; *"perchance"* → *"perhaps."*
- Inverted or archaic syntax used as ornament in otherwise modern prose is also Wardour Street: *"What think you of this?"* instead of *"What do you think of this?"*; *"Heard you not his answer?"* instead of *"Did you not hear his answer?"* These belong only in a consistent archaic register.
- Distinguish from old words that remain in current formal use: *nevertheless, notwithstanding, albeit, forthwith, hitherto, heretofore, therein* — these are old but not archaic; they appear in contemporary formal and legal writing and are not Wardour Street unless used in a self-consciously antique way.

## Objective Rubric

- No word in the revision belongs to an archaic register that the rest of the prose does not sustain throughout.
- Every archaic word removed has been replaced by a modern equivalent of equal or greater precision, or cut without loss of meaning.
- No form from the Wardour-Street list (*forsooth, methinks, ere, prithee, perchance, mayhap, betimes, anon, certes, marry, hight, yclept, 'tis, 'twas*) remains in a passage otherwise written in modern English.
- No meaning has been lost by removing the archaic forms.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
