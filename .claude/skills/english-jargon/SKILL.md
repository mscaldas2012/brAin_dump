# English Jargon

Use this skill when reviewing or rewriting prose where abstraction, passive construction, or circumlocution is the controlling problem.

Source concept: Jargon is to be perpetually shuffling around in the fog and cotton-wool of abstract terms — preferring abstract nouns to concrete ones and the stationary passive to the active transitive verb, so that the sentence never quite lands on its subject. (Quiller-Couch, *On the Art of Writing*, Lecture V, 1916)

## Rules

- Use transitive verbs that strike their object. Eliminate the **stationary passive** — passive constructions built on chains of auxiliaries and participles (*was made the recipient of*, *is being given consideration to*, *has been brought to the attention of*).
- Replace abstract noun constructions with verbs wherever an action is described: "had losses or gains" → "won or lost"; "was made the recipient of a silver teapot" → "received a silver teapot"; "brought to a satisfactory conclusion" → "finished."
- Remove jargon words on sight: *case, instance, nature, condition, degree, as regards, with regard to, in respect of, in connection with, the fact that.* These are circumlocutions for direct statement and should never appear.
- Apply the reduction test: rewrite the sentence at half its length. If no meaning is lost, the original was jargon.
- Prefer concrete nouns — words that name things — over abstract nouns that name categories or concepts of things.

## Objective Rubric

- Every verb in the revision is transitive and active, or is intransitive with a clear subject performing an action — not a stationary passive.
- No jargon words remain: *case, instance, nature, condition, degree, as regards, with regard to, in respect of, in connection with.*
- Abstract noun constructions that express actions are replaced by verbs.
- The revision is no longer than necessary; the original was longer without being clearer or more precise.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.

## Source Boundary

Examples should be drawn from public-domain English prose (pre-1928) or invented passages clearly labeled as such. Do not invent quotations attributed to named authors. When a task supplies a passage, diagnose it directly without substituting a different example.
