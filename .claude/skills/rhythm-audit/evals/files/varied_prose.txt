The novel opens in the middle of an argument.

Not a dramatic one — no raised voices, no slammed doors — but the quiet, flattening kind that couples learn to conduct entirely through implication, through the slight edge in a question, the too-careful way one person picks up a glass. By page three we understand, without being told, that this marriage has been failing for years.

What Ferrante does in the first chapter that most novelists wouldn't dare is refuse to explain. She gives us the argument without context. We don't know who started it, or why, or what the stakes are. We are dropped in mid-sentence, mid-feeling, and expected to orient ourselves by paying attention.

This is actually the harder task for the reader — and the more rewarding one.

Most literary fiction announces its intentions early. Here is a family, here is its fracture, here is what the novel will ask of you. Ferrante withholds the frame. She trusts that confusion, properly handled, creates a kind of forward pressure, a need to read that no amount of beautiful prose can manufacture on its own.

The gamble pays off. By the time context arrives, we're already inside the marriage, already taking sides.
