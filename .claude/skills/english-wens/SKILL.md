# English Wens

Use this skill when a sentence part — a subordinate clause, parenthetical, modifier chain, or relative clause — has swelled beyond proportion to the sentence that houses it, even when the swollen part contains information the writer considers necessary.

Source concept: A wen is a benign growth on the body of a sentence — a phrase or clause that has expanded well beyond what its structural role justifies, throwing the sentence out of proportion in the manner of a cyst that has grown larger than the part of the face it occupies. The information may be real; the bulk is the problem. (Fowler & Fowler, *The King's English*, 1908)

## Rules

- **The proportion test**: a wen is present when a modifying, parenthetical, or subordinate element is markedly longer than the main clause it belongs to, or when an interrupting element is longer than either clause it interrupts. Count roughly: if the modifier outweighs its host by two to one or more, suspect a wen.
- **Signs of a wen**: (a) a relative clause that runs to three or more sub-clauses of its own before the main predicate appears; (b) an em-dash parenthetical that contains information which could stand as its own sentence; (c) a preparatory adverbial clause so elaborate that the reader has lost the grammatical thread by the time the main verb arrives; (d) a sentence where the main predicate ("she said," "he left") is buried in the middle, surrounded by qualification on both sides.
- **Treatments**: (a) extract the wen into a separate sentence — this is the most common repair; (b) compress the wen to the essential point and cut the rest; (c) demote the wen to a footnote or parenthetical reference if the information is genuinely supplementary; (d) if the wen contains nothing the reader needs, cut it entirely.
- Distinguish from a wen and a darling: a darling (see `english-murder-your-darlings`) is kept from fondness — the problem is motivation. A wen may contain necessary information — the problem is proportion. The repairs differ: a darling is cut; a wen is extracted, compressed, or relocated.
- Distinguish from omit-needless-words: omit-needless-words removes surplus language throughout a sentence. The wen skill addresses structural imbalance — even a tightly written subordinate clause can be a wen if it is disproportionately large.

## Objective Rubric

- No modifying or parenthetical element in the revision outweighs its host main clause by a ratio of two to one or more.
- No interrupting parenthetical contains more than one subordinate clause of its own; more complex material is a separate sentence.
- The main verb and its subject are reachable from the sentence's opening without passing through more than one interrupting element.
- Any information extracted from the wen is either present in a separate sentence, demoted to a note, or confirmed as not needed.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
