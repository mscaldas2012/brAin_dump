# English End Emphasis

Use this skill when a sentence's most important word or phrase is not in the position of greatest emphasis — when the key point arrives too early, is buried in the middle, or is followed by a trailing qualifier that drains the sentence's energy.

Source concept: "The proper place in the sentence for the word, or group of words, which the writer desires to make most prominent is usually the end." The end of the sentence receives the greatest stress; the opening receives the second-greatest; the middle is the weakest position of all. (Strunk, *The Elements of Style*, 1918)

## Rules

- **End position = greatest emphasis**: the last word or phrase of a sentence receives the most stress when read, and is most likely to be retained. If the sentence's most important element is not last, the sentence should be restructured.
- **Opening position = second emphasis**: the grammatical subject and the sentence's first phrase also carry weight. Use the opening position deliberately — for contrast, for the topic being developed, or for an urgently stated condition. Do not waste either position on weak function words or dispensable qualifiers.
- **Middle = weakest position**: important words buried in the middle of a long sentence are most likely to be overlooked. Move them to the end or the opening.
- **Trailing qualifiers drain emphasis**: a sentence that ends in *"or so I thought," "in a sense," "as it turned out," "at least in part," "to some degree,"* or similar hedges weakens the sentence's landing. Move the qualifier to the opening, or cut it if it is evasive. *"She was right, as it turned out"* → *"As it turned out, she was right."*
- **The "there is" problem of emphasis**: expletive constructions (*there is, it was*) place the real subject in a weak middle position. Restructure to open on the real subject or to end on the real predicate. (See also `english-active-voice`.)
- Do not apply mechanically: a sentence may intentionally end on a weak word for rhythmic or rhetorical reasons — an anticlimactic ending can be an effect rather than an error. The test is intentionality: is the ending deliberate or accidental?

## Objective Rubric

- The most important word or phrase in each sentence occupies the end position, or a deliberate alternative placement is explicitly justified.
- No sentence ends in a hedging qualifier (*or so I thought, in a sense, at least in part, to some degree*) where the qualifier could be moved to the opening or cut.
- No important element is surrounded by subordinate material on both sides with no resolution at the sentence's end.
- Where a trailing qualifier was moved to the opening, the opening now sets a frame that the sentence's ending fulfills.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
