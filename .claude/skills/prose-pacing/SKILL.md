# Prose Pacing

Use this skill when a piece feels relentless or breathless — when the reader's attention is demanded without relief — or when a piece loses energy in its second half and arrives at its close with less force than it opened with. Both are pacing failures, and both are diagnosable from structure alone, without reading every word.

**Minimum text unit:** Structural skeleton — section headers (or opening phrases where there are no headers), opening sentence, closing sentence, and approximate word count per section. Pacing analysis does not require the full body of each section. This skill can be applied at all text tiers, including book-length texts, from the skeleton alone.

Source concept: "It is obvious that prose has no tide. Each sentence must be self-supporting; must act as its own motor; must carry the reader forward solely by its own energy." (Quiller-Couch, *On the Art of Writing*, Lecture VI, 1916.) What is true of the sentence holds at the section level: a section that exceeds its carrying capacity does not gain force from the extra words — it loses it. Prose cannot coast on the momentum of its form; every unit must pull its weight. Aristotle applies the proportionality principle to the whole discourse: "each part ought to be of the proper length" — neither so short that it cannot do its work, nor so long that it overflows its function. (*Rhetoric*, Book III Ch. 17.) Forster distinguishes "rhythm" — felt in the reading — from "pattern" — seen in retrospect. Pacing manages rhythm: the reader's experience of demand and relief as they move through the text. (*Aspects of the Novel*, Chapter 8, 1927.) A piece without rhythm can be admirable sentence by sentence and exhausting as a whole.

## Density Weight

Before mapping, assign each section a density weight. These are relative judgments within a given piece, not absolute categories.

**Heavy** — introduces and develops multiple substantial ideas, or develops one complex idea requiring sustained attention. The reader must slow down and engage actively.

**Medium** — introduces and develops one substantial idea, event, or claim. The reader pays attention but is not heavily taxed.

**Light** — introduces little that is new; the section is primarily anecdote, brief example, transitional narrative, or a single assertion without development. The reader moves through it at pace.

**Transitional** — exists to move the reader between sections or registers; primary function is connective, not substantive. Typically short.

The diagnostic question is always relative: given the surrounding sections and the demands of this genre, what does this section ask of the reader compared to its neighbors?

## Fault Taxonomy

Four named faults.

### Fault 1 — Density Pile-Up
Three or more consecutive sections that are Heavy, with no Light or Transitional section between them. The reader's sustained attention is demanded without interruption. The problem is not the density of any single section but the distribution — each heavy section would be acceptable in isolation. Together, they exhaust without replenishing.

*Test:* Scan the pacing map for any run of three or more consecutive Heavy sections. If found, the run is a pile-up regardless of the individual quality of those sections.

*Repair path:* Identify where a light section, anecdote, example, or transitional passage could be inserted or expanded. Or reorder so that no more than two Heavy sections appear consecutively without a lighter one between them.

### Fault 2 — Over-Inflation
A section is substantially longer than its content can sustain. The central idea could be expressed in half (or less) the current word count without losing any claim, narrative beat, or essential information. The extra length is filled with restatement, accumulating examples past the point of illustration, or hedging and qualification that exceeds the argument's actual uncertainty.

*Test:* Compress the section's argument or content into a single sentence. Now count the section's claims and sub-claims. If there is one core idea and the section runs more than three times the length needed to state and develop it once, apply the compression test: could the section be halved without losing a distinct claim? If yes, it is over-inflated.

*Distinction:* A long section that introduces and develops multiple genuine ideas is not over-inflated; it may be dense (Fault 1 risk) but its length is earned by its content. Over-inflation applies specifically to sections that are long relative to a single idea.

### Fault 3 — Gear Change Without Signal
An abrupt shift in section length or density that violates the reader's pace expectation without any tonal or rhetorical signal that a shift is happening. The reader is moving at one speed and is required to shift without warning.

*Signs:* A short, light section immediately following several long, heavy ones with no transition (no scene break, no change in register, no direct address). Or the reverse: a dense, compressed section inserted into a sequence of lighter, expansive ones.

*Distinction:* Deliberate gear changes are often effective — a sudden short section after a long one can land with real force. The fault is the absence of signal. When the shift is marked (a white space break, a tonal shift, a change of address), the reader adjusts; when it is unmarked, they stumble. Identify whether the shift has been signaled or not.

### Fault 4 — Energy Deficit
The piece's second half is markedly lighter — in density, argumentative force, or narrative intensity — than its first half. The piece peaks too early. The reader arrives at the conclusion with the sense that the argument ran out of momentum rather than that it arrived.

*Test:* Assign each section to the first or second half of the piece. Compare average density weights across the two halves. If the second half is consistently lighter, identify the section where the energy shift occurs and ask: is the piece designed to decelerate toward a quiet close, or does the second half need to carry weight (evidence, proof, narrative complication, resolution) that it is currently failing to carry?

*Distinction:* Deliberate deceleration toward a quiet ending is not an energy deficit. The fault applies when the second half should be substantive but is instead lighter than the first, suggesting the writer ran out of energy rather than choosing restraint. The close of an argument should typically be at least as dense as its opening.

## Review Shape

```
Pacing Map:
Section                   | ~words | Density      | Function
<name or opening phrase>  | ~NNN   | Heavy        | <what it does: develops argument X, introduces concept Y>
<name or opening phrase>  | ~NNN   | Medium       | <what it does>
<name or opening phrase>  | ~NNN   | Light        | <what it does: anecdote, example, transition>
<name or opening phrase>  | ~NNN   | Transitional | <what it does>
... (all sections)

Distribution:
<one paragraph describing the overall pattern — where the weight is concentrated, whether there is relief, whether the piece breathes or is relentless, whether the second half holds its energy>

Faults:
1. [Fault type] at [location]: <description of the specific structural problem>
2. [Fault type] at [location]: <description>
...

Repair:
<for each fault: a structural recommendation — not a line-level rewrite, but a structural action: insert a light section here, compress this section, signal the gear change with a scene break or tonal shift, move this heavy section earlier to rebalance the second half>
```

## Objective Rubric

- Every section appears in the pacing map with a density weight and a function note — no section omitted.
- The distribution paragraph describes the overall pattern and names where the weight is concentrated.
- Each named fault includes a location and a description of the structural problem.
- Repair recommendations are structural (add, compress, reorder, signal) rather than line-level rewrites.
- Any run of three or more consecutive Heavy sections in the pacing map is flagged as Fault 1, or explicitly cleared with a note on why the pile-up is appropriate in this genre or at this point in the text.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
