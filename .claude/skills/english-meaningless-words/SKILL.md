# English Meaningless Words

Use this skill when words in a critical, evaluative, or argumentative passage create the impression of meaning without delivering it — particularly in literary criticism, art writing, and political prose.

Source concept: Meaningless words do not point to any discoverable object; they are not even expected to do so by the reader. They function as emotional signals — praise or condemnation without content — and their presence marks the point where thought has stopped. (Orwell, "Politics and the English Language," 1946)

## Rules

- **The content test:** for every evaluative or descriptive word, ask whether the writer could define it in a way that excludes its opposite. If *romantic* can mean either high emotion or low feeling depending on the critic's mood, it is meaningless as used. If *democratic* is applied to any regime the writer approves of, it is meaningless. Words that cannot be defined in a way that distinguishes them from their contraries have stopped being words and become emotional gestures.
- **Critical and aesthetic vocabulary** prone to emptiness: *romantic, classical, vital, human, dead, sentimental, natural, organic, authentic, visceral, raw, honest, challenging, important, significant, interesting, relevant.* These words are not always empty — they can carry content. But they are used so often as pure tone-markers that every occurrence requires scrutiny.
- **Political and ideological vocabulary** prone to emptiness: *democratic, progressive, reactionary, freedom, justice, patriotic, fascist, radical.* These words carry "several different meanings which cannot be reconciled with one another" and are routinely used with one private definition while allowing readers to apply another. Specify what is actually meant, or use a more precise term.
- The remedy is **specification**: replace the general evaluative term with a concrete description of the quality, property, or action it supposedly denotes. Instead of *"the novel's authentic voice,"* identify what makes the voice authentic — the diction, the relationship between speaker and subject, the absence of evasion. Instead of *"a visionary leader,"* state what she envisioned and what she did about it.
- Distinguish meaningless words from legitimate abstractions: *justice, freedom, authentic* are not inherently empty — they become empty when used without a committed definition. A writer who specifies exactly what they mean by the term is using it legitimately, even if the term is large.

## Objective Rubric

- Every evaluative term in the revision passes the content test: it is defined in a way that would exclude its opposite as used in this context.
- No critical or aesthetic term (*authentic, vital, honest, important, challenging, significant*) appears without a concrete description of the quality it names.
- No political or ideological term (*democratic, progressive, reactionary*) appears without a definition or specific referent that distinguishes this use from its contraries.
- The revision identifies properties, actions, or qualities that the original terms were gesturing at but not naming.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
