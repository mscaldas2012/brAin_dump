# English Pretentious Diction

Use this skill when vocabulary has been chosen for the impression it makes rather than for the precision with which it names the thing — when words are doing status work instead of meaning work.

Source concept: Pretentious diction uses words to dress up simple statements and give an air of scientific impartiality to biased judgements, of historic grandeur to political events, or of cultural sophistication to ordinary observations — driven by the mistaken belief that Latin or Greek words are grander than Saxon ones. (Orwell, "Politics and the English Language," 1946)

## Rules

- **Pattern 1 — Pseudo-scientific Latinate words** simulate academic precision: *utilize* (use), *facilitate* (help or allow), *implement* (carry out or do), *individual* as a noun for person, *constitute* (make up), *exhibit* (show), *parameter* (limit or range), *methodology* (method), *leverage* (use or exploit). Test: is there a shorter, plainer English word that does the same job? If yes, use it.
- **Pattern 2 — Grandeur-inflation adjectives** elevate ordinary events to historic or epic significance: *epoch-making, landmark, pivotal, seminal, iconic, game-changing, transformative, visionary, unprecedented, watershed.* Test: would this adjective survive a sceptical editor asking "says who?" If a development is genuinely historic, say specifically what makes it so — do not use the adjective as a substitute for the evidence.
- **Pattern 3 — Foreign phrases as culture signals** are used not because they are the shortest path to the meaning but to display learning: *ancien régime, mutatis mutandis, inter alia, vis-à-vis* (in non-legal contexts), *Weltanschauung, locus, milieu* (when plain equivalents exist). Test: is there a plain English equivalent? If yes, use it. Genuine exceptions: terms with no adequate English equivalent in standard educated use, or technical terms required by the field.
- The **diagnostic test** across all three patterns: was this word chosen because it most precisely names the thing, or because it sounds more authoritative, educated, or important than the alternatives? If the latter, it is pretentious diction.
- Latinate words are not always pretentious — precision sometimes requires them. The fault is choosing them *for their Latin-ness* rather than their precision.

## Objective Rubric

- Every Latinate or Greek word in the revision either (a) has no adequate shorter English equivalent, or (b) is replaced by a plainer word.
- No grandeur-inflation adjective (*historic, epoch-making, transformative, iconic, pivotal*) appears without specific evidence in the passage for the claim.
- No foreign phrase appears where a plain English construction would serve as well.
- Each word in the revision names the thing as precisely as possible with the shortest available word that does the job.

Pass only when every applicable check passes.
